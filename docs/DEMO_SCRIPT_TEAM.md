# ORIGIN-T demo: three-person script

About 13 minutes. One story, three voices:

- **Revanth: the simple version.** What problem, what the tool does, what the answer means, in plain words. He **drives the laptop** for the whole demo.
- **Siddharth: the workflow.** Where the data comes from, how an experiment is built, how a bad dataset gets repaired, and how the team worked.
- **Vishnu: the technical depth.** What the audit measures, how much to trust it, and the three research questions around it (training-time alarm, scaling, gates).

**The story:** a dataset can quietly damage a model. *Can we tell before we train? Can we see it while training? Can we fix it?*

Every number is in a committed report (named in brackets). Say the failures out loud: they are the credibility.

## 1. Revanth: the problem (0:00-1:15), landing page `/`
**[SHOW]** The page loads; the chart draws in. Do not click yet.

**REVANTH:** "Training a language model on a dataset is cheap to start and expensive to undo. Today the only way to learn whether a dataset will hurt your model is to train on it, and that can take days.

And the damage can be quiet. When a model learns from text that other models wrote, the rare things in the language disappear first. People call that model collapse.

We built ORIGIN-T to answer one question: *before you train, can you tell whether this dataset will damage your model?*

This chart is our first answer, and it is real data, not an illustration. Each line is a kind of contaminated text mixed into normal human text, from none on the left to all of it on the right. Up means the model got worse. Generated text pushes the line up. The grey line is our control: we swapped in the same amount of fresh human text, and it stays flat. So the damage comes from the contamination, not from just changing the data.

My teammates will show you where this evidence comes from, then we will try the tool live."

**[CUE]** "Siddharth, how did we get that ground truth?"

## 2. Siddharth: ground truth (1:15-3:00), `/curves`
**[CUE to Revanth]** "Revanth, open Collapse curves."  **[CLICK]** nav "Collapse curves".

**SIDDHARTH:** "To teach a tool to spot bad data you need data where you *know* the answer. So we made it.

The workflow is the same for every experiment. One: take a draw of clean human text from WikiText-2 and split it in two halves. One half is the training data. The other half is the raw material for contamination, so a contaminated sample never shares text with a clean one. Two: make five kinds of contamination from that material: generated by a model, recursively generated, paraphrased, leaked benchmark items, and duplicates. Every sample carries its true origin as a label. Three: mix a known share into the training set, from zero to a hundred percent, always 1,000 samples, so the only thing that changes is the contamination. Four: fine-tune a small model and measure it on text it has never seen.

We did that for 75 cells on WikiText and 105 on TinyStories, three random seeds each. That is what you are looking at."

**[CLICK]** untick `human_control`, tick it again. **[SAY]** "Colour is the contamination type. The line is the average over seeds, the band is the spread, so you can see the noise. The flat one is the human control."

**[CLICK]** the Metric dropdown, choose benchmark.
"Leaked test items behave differently. They barely move the held-out score, but the model gets suspiciously good at the leaked items themselves. That is memorisation, not degradation, so it needs its own metric."

**[CLICK]** one dot. "Every dot opens that exact run, with its features and the prediction the risk model made *before* training, next to what actually happened."

**SIDDHARTH:** "Two safeguards worth knowing. A hidden holdout, carved by text hash, that no stage is allowed to train on. And pools are versioned, so a fix to a generator can never silently mix two generators in one curve. We learned that the hard way: our first leaked-benchmark generator recased text and made the model look *worse*, not better, until we caught it."

**[CUE]** "Revanth, show them what the tool does with a dataset."

## 4. Vishnu: what the audit measures (4:00-5:30), `/try` step 2
**[SHOW]** Stay on the analysis screen while the stages step through. Point at each as it lights up.

**VISHNU:** "These are the audit's real stages, and the time next to each is what the server actually spent. They are held on screen briefly so a fast run is followable, but the times are not pacing.

First it computes features: 22 numbers that describe the whole dataset. Lexical statistics: how diverse the vocabulary is, how much it repeats. A leak check against benchmark items. Then the one that matters most: a **reference language model**, a fixed GPT-2. Machine-written text is unusually predictable to another language model, so the spread of its loss across the dataset is the strongest single signal we found. Then sentence embeddings, to see whether texts crowd together and whether rare topics survive.

Then the **risk model**: a logistic regression over those 22 features, trained on our grid cells, that outputs the probability the dataset degrades a model. On top of it sits **split conformal prediction**, which turns that probability into a set of possible outcomes with a stated coverage. It is allowed to answer 'uncertain'.

Last, per-sample scores, so we can point at the individual records to blame, not just the dataset."

**[WHEN DONE]** the page moves to results by itself.

**[CUE]** "Revanth, read us the answer."

## 6. Vishnu: how much to trust it (7:00-8:00), caveat and `/overview`
**[POINT]** the grey line under the verdict.

**VISHNU:** "That line is read live from our evaluation, and it is the honest part. On contamination types the model had never seen, its AUROC is 0.83. But its conformal sets covered the true outcome 81 percent of the time against a 90 percent target, so they under-cover. And within a single dataset it is no better than one reference-model feature, so we do not claim the full multi-feature model beats a simple detector. It also misses leaked benchmark items entirely, which is why the leak flag exists separately. Treat it as a screen, not a guarantee."

**[CUE]** "Revanth, open Evidence." **[CLICK]** nav "Evidence".

**VISHNU:** "Every row is a test whose pass-or-fail rule was written and merged *before* its result existed. Passed: the collapse is real and grows with dose on both datasets, the feature suite and risk model run on both, and a small model predicts a bigger one. Failed: early warning on the contamination axis, twice, and the live alarm. We report those as written."

## 7. Vishnu: can we see it while training? (8:00-9:30), `/monitor`
**[CLICK]** nav "Live monitor", pick a contaminated run, replay it.

**VISHNU:** "Second question: during training, can the model's own insides warn us? We log six signals every ten steps, things like how spread out the internal representations are, and compare them to a band learned from healthy runs. The alarm is calibrated so a healthy run triggers it at most 5 percent of the time. This is a replay; it streams live while a run trains."

**[POINT]** the signal charts and band, then the alarm event. "The band is healthy behaviour, the alarm is when a run leaves it."

**VISHNU:** "One surprise: the loss never goes up inside a run. A contaminated run just improves less than a healthy one. So 'alarm when the loss rises' would never fire. The signals do move earlier: one of them, anisotropy, separates the two arms by step 20 of 125.

The honest result: the alarm fired before the damage on 8 of 18 collapsing runs, 44 percent against a bar of 50. One of 16 healthy runs false-alarmed, 6 percent, inside the bar. Every miss was a run already damaged at the first logged step. We did not move the bar."

**[CUE]** "Revanth, open Canary."

## 8. Vishnu: does it scale? (9:30-10:15), `/canary`
**VISHNU:** "A fair objection: 82 million parameters is a toy. So we trained a bigger model, Pythia-410M, on exactly the same data, and asked whether the small model's damage predicts the big one's."

**[POINT]** the scatter. "One dot per matched run. Across the x axis, how much the small model was hurt. Up the y axis, how much the big one was. Rank correlation 0.78, with an interval of 0.51 to 0.91, which cleared our bar of 0.7. Part of that is just more contamination hurting both, and the page says so."

**[CUE]** "Siddharth, what do you do about a bad dataset?"

## 9. Siddharth: can we fix it? (10:15-12:30), `/repair`, `/bench`, and how we worked
**[CUE to Revanth]** "Revanth, open Repair." **[CLICK]** nav "Repair".

**SIDDHARTH:** "If the audit says a dataset is bad, can we repair it? The workflow: score every record for harm, replace the 500 worst with clean ones, retrain, and measure how much of the lost quality comes back. A hundred percent means back to clean."

**[POINT]** the strategy columns. "Each column is a strategy. Random is the floor; oracle, which knows the true labels, is the reference point. Harm-guided, our method, uses gradients to find the records that hurt the model most."

**SIDDHARTH:** "It works: harm-guided beats random by 25 to 55 points on all nine cases, and we replicated the full grid on independent data. But the honest finding is that cleaning by *origin*, simply removing text that looks machine-written, does about as well. Harm minus origin is minus 0.03, so a tie. And on leaked benchmark items harm-guided repair recovers nothing, while a plain near-duplicate score matches the oracle. We also tested a rule to protect rare topics while cleaning. It changed nothing measurable."

**[CUE]** "Revanth, open Bench." **[CLICK]** nav "Bench".

**SIDDHARTH:** "The same data is the benchmark. Every training set the grid used is browsable by type and ratio, with hashes and the exact generator versions, so anyone can test their own detector against ground truth. We are packaging it for public release under CC BY-SA, inherited from WikiText."

**SIDDHARTH (how we worked):** "One last thing about how this was built, because it is why you can trust the numbers. Three tracks, one handbook as the single source of truth. Every task was a GitHub issue; every change a pull request that had to pass tests and lint. And the rule that mattered: for each experiment the pass-or-fail rule was merged *before* we computed its verdict. When the early-warning rule failed, we reported it, and wrote down the one thing we could not hide: our second dataset had been run before the rule merged, and we say so in the paper."

**[CUE]** "Revanth, to close."

**REVANTH:** "So, in short. We built a testbed where the answer is known. On top of it, a tool that looks at a dataset before you train and tells you how likely it is to hurt your model, and which records to remove first. Damage grows with how much contamination there is, a small model predicts a bigger one, and one simple signal, how predictable the text is, carries most of it.

## Who answers which question
| Question | Answers | Short answer |
|---|---|---|
| "Isn't this already published?" | Vishnu | Predicting fine-tuning outcomes is (TuneAhead, ICML 2026). Ours is narrower: damage *caused by contamination*, with conformal sets, against ground truth. A pre-training contamination score exists too (SynthSentry); it does not tie to damage. |
| "Why did the early-warning gate fail?" | Vishnu | On WikiText, 9 of 12 contaminated runs already collapse at the first level, so there is no earlier stage. The TinyStories run with finer levels still found no signal above the baseline (AUROC 0.08-0.56). Reported, not tuned. |
| "Is the risk model better than plain GPT-2 loss?" | Vishnu | Pooled, yes; within one dataset, no, they are within 0.03 AUROC. We do not claim it. |
| "Does it work on my model?" | Vishnu | Not shown. Validated on DistilGPT-2 + LoRA and one 410M target. |
| "How do you know the data is clean and unleaked?" | Siddharth | Hidden holdout by text hash, leak checks on id and normalised text, versioned pools, and the bench refuses to regenerate a missing pool. |
| "Why not just remove machine-written text?" | Siddharth | You can, and it works as well as our harm-based repair. That is our finding, not a failure. |
| "What do I do with the result?" | Revanth | Remove the flagged samples first; if it says uncertain, do not trust either way and test on a small run. |
| "Can I try it?" | Revanth | Yes: upload a file or use a sample; the page explains each step. |
| "How long does it take?" | Revanth | About 3 minutes per 1,000 records on a GPU; the first run after a restart loads the models. |

## If something goes wrong
- **The audit seems stuck on the first stage:** the models are loading. Vishnu keeps talking through section 4; it clears within about a minute. This is why you warm the server first.
- **The API is down:** the landing page still loads and shows a placeholder frame. Restart it (`python -m dashboard.api`) and reload; Vishnu talks through the eight stages from memory while it comes back.
- **A page looks empty:** it means a report is missing on this machine. Say "that screen needs the grid reports" and move on; do not apologise at length.
- **Over time:** cut section 8 (Canary) first, then the Bench half of section 9.
